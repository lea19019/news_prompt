Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promising results in news classification tasks through
prompting, yet systematic comparisons of prompt engineering techniques on open-source models remain
limited. This study evaluates five prompt engineering techniques, zero-shot, few-shot, constrained output,
chain-of-thought, and self-consistency, across eight recent open-source LLMs on three benchmark news
classification datasets. We investigate which prompting strategies yield optimal performance for singlelabel news classification and how effectiveness varies across model architectures and dataset
characteristics. Our experimental pipeline will be held using Ollama in the CS 653 GPUs, it will enable
comprehensive evaluation across 120+ model-prompt-dataset combinations. Results will provide evidencebased guidance for practitioners selecting prompting strategies for classification tasks with open-source
models, contributing both empirical benchmarks and practical recommendations to the growing body of
prompt engineering research
1. Introduction
News classification through prompting has
emerged as a practical alternative to fine-tuning
for deploying large language models. Recent
work has shown that carefully designed prompts
can elicit strong classification performance from
pretrained models without task-specific training.
However, most studies have focused on
proprietary models like GPT-4 or evaluated only
specific prompting techniques in isolation. The
rapid release of open-source LLMs in 2024 and
2025, spanning diverse architectures from
compact 4B-parameter models to 24B-parameter
systems, creates an urgent need for systematic
evaluation of prompt engineering techniques
across these models.
The challenge for practitioners is clear: given a
classification task and an open-source model,
which prompting strategy should be used? Zeroshot prompts are simple but may underperform.
Few-shot examples improve accuracy but
require careful selection. Constrained output
formats reduce parsing errors but may limit
model expressiveness. Chain-of-thought
prompting shows promise for reasoning tasks
but adds computational overhead. Selfconsistency aggregates multiple predictions for
robustness but multiplies inference costs.
Without empirical comparisons across models
and datasets, practitioners resort to trial-anderror, wasting development time and compute
resources.
This work provides an evaluation of five prompt
engineering techniques across eight recent opensource LLMs on three news classification
benchmarks. We examine how model
architecture (general-purpose vs. reasoningspecialized), size (4B to 24B parameters), and
dataset complexity (4-class to 20-class
categorization) affect prompt effectiveness. Our
contributions include: (1) empirical benchmarks
comparing prompting strategies across 120+
experimental conditions, (2) analysis of
accuracy-cost trade-offs for different
approaches, and (3) practical recommendations
for selecting prompts based on model
characteristics and task requirements.
2. Methodology
2.1 Datasets
We evaluate prompt techniques across three
benchmark news classification datasets
representing different granularities and domains.
The AG News dataset contains 120,000 training
articles and 7,600 test articles categorized into
four classes: Sports, Business,
Science/Technology, and World news. This
dataset provides a baseline for coarse-grained
classification with clear semantic boundaries
between categories. The BBC News dataset
comprises approximately 2,225 articles across
five categories: Business, Entertainment,
Politics, Sport, and Technology. This
intermediate-complexity dataset introduces finer
distinctions, particularly between Business and
Technology categories that may overlap. The 20
Newsgroups dataset presents the greatest
challenge with approximately 18,000 documents
spanning twenty distinct topics in discussion
forum format, requiring the model to distinguish
between closely related categories.
These datasets provide varying levels of
classification difficulty, from coarse-grained
four-class categorization to fine-grained twentyclass distinction. This range allows us to assess
how prompt techniques perform across different
task complexities and whether strategies that
succeed on simple tasks scale to more
challenging scenarios. The BBC News dataset
example below illustrates the classification task
structure:
text label label_text
wales want rugby
league…
2 sport
china aviation
seeks rescue…
1 business
rock band u2
break ticket…
3 entertainment

2.2 Models
We evaluate eight recent open-source LLMs
spanning multiple architectures, sizes, and
design philosophies. The selection includes
Gemma3 at both 4B and 12B parameters from
Google, representing compact and balanced
configurations. Microsoft's Phi-4 at 14B
parameters emphasizes reasoning capabilities.
OpenAI's GPT-OSS at 20B parameters
represents their first open-weight model release.
Alibaba's Qwen3 at 8B parameters brings strong
multilingual support. Mistral's Small 3.2 at 24B
parameters represents the largest model in our
evaluation. DeepSeek-R1 at 8B parameters
offers a reasoning-specialized architecture, while
OLMo2 at 7B parameters provides a fully open
alternative with transparent training data and
methodology.
These models were selected based on several
criteria. First, all models were released between
2024 and 2025, ensuring we evaluate
contemporary architectures. Second, all models
support news classification tasks and can be
deployed via Ollama on our class GPUs
infrastructure. Third, the parameter range from
4B to 24B allows us to examine how model
scale interacts with prompting technique
effectiveness. Fourth, the architectural diversity
enables comparisons between general-purpose
models, reasoning-specialized models, and
multilingual models. Complete model
specifications are provided in Appendix B.
2.3 Prompt Engineering Techniques
We evaluate five core prompt engineering
techniques drawn from the comprehensive
taxonomy presented in "The Prompt Report" by
Schulhoff et al. [1]. Each technique represents a
distinct approach to eliciting classification
behavior, progressing from simple instructionbased prompts to more complex ensemble
methods.
Zero-shot prompting provides only task
instructions without examples, relying entirely
on the model's pre-existing capabilities. Our
template explicitly enumerates valid categories
to reduce hallucination of invalid labels. This
design follows findings by Sun et al. [3], who
observed 8-12% accuracy improvements when
categories are explicitly listed versus openended questions.
Few-shot prompting includes three labeled
examples before the target classification
instance, leveraging in-context learning through
input-output demonstrations. We selected three
examples based on context length constraints
across our model range and evidence from
Brown et al. (2020) showing diminishing returns
beyond 3-5 examples for classification tasks.
Examples are stratified across different
categories and remain fixed across all test
instances to ensure reproducibility. Each
example is short (5-7 words), unambiguous, and
presented in dash-list format with arrow notation
(→) separating input from output.
Constrained output extends few-shot
prompting by explicitly restricting response
format. The prompt instructs models to "Answer
with ONLY ONE WORD from: [category list]"
to address two common failure modes: verbose
explanations and invalid category names. This
approach follows structured output generation
research [5], which found explicit format
constraints reduce parsing errors by 15-30%.
Chain-of-thought prompting encourages
explicit reasoning before classification through a
three-step process: identify the main topic,
determine the best category, and provide the
answer. Wei et al. (2022) demonstrated that CoT
improves performance on reasoning tasks by
articulating intermediate steps. However, its
effectiveness for straightforward classification
versus complex multi-step reasoning remains an
open empirical question. The structured steps
provide consistency compared to free-form
"explain your reasoning" approaches.
Self-consistency implements an ensemble
approach by generating five independent 
predictions with temperature 0.7 and selecting
the category via majority voting. Wang et al.
(2022) found 10-20% accuracy improvements
on reasoning tasks using this technique. We use
five samples to balance robustness with
computational cost (5× inference time),
following standard self-consistency
recommendations. Temperature 0.7 provides
sufficient diversity for meaningful voting while
avoiding excessive randomness.
Complete prompt templates are provided in
Appendix C.
2.4 Experimental Setup
All experiments will be conducted on the class
GPUs using Ollama. This infrastructure provides
consistent hardware conditions across all
evaluations, eliminating confounding variables
from heterogeneous computing environments.
We will implement an automated evaluation
pipeline that systematically processes each
dataset-model-prompt combination. For each
dataset, the pipeline loads the test set and
preprocesses the text. It then generates prompts
according to each technique's template, for fewshot methods, we select three representative
training examples covering different categories
and keep these fixed across all test instances.
The pipeline will query the models via the
Ollama API using temperature 0.0 for
deterministic techniques (zero-shot, few-shot,
constrained output, chain-of-thought) and
temperature 0.7 for self-consistency.
Model outputs will be parsed to extract predicted
categories using pattern matching that handles
common response variations. For selfconsistency, we generate five independent
predictions per instance and select the category
via majority voting. The pipeline will record all
predictions, inference latencies, and parsing
success rates in structured format.
The experimental scope encompasses
approximately 120 evaluation conditions (8
models × 5 prompts × 3 datasets). To ensure
reproducibility, we will fix random seeds, use
identical test sets across all models, and
maintain consistent prompt templates that vary
only in category labels and domain terminology.
2.5 Evaluation Metrics
We will evaluate classification performance
using four standard metrics:
• Accuracy: Measures the proportion of
correctly classified instances, providing
an overall performance summary.
• Precision: Quantifies the proportion of
predicted positives that are true positives
for each class.
• Recall: Measures the proportion of
actual positives correctly identified for
each class.
• F1-Score: Computes the harmonic
mean of precision and recall for each
class, then averages these scores across
all classes (macro-averaging), ensuring
that all classes contribute equally to the
final metric regardless of their frequency
in the test set.
These metrics provide complementary
perspectives on model performance. While
accuracy offers a single-number summary, it can
be misleading when class distributions are
imbalanced. Precision and recall reveal how
models perform on individual categories—for
instance, whether they confidently predict
common categories while struggling with rare
ones. Macro-averaged F1 is particularly
important for datasets like BBC News where
category sizes may vary, as it prevents
performance on majority classes from
dominating the evaluation.
